Craft Log: Machining Custom 301 High-Yield Picks with Brass Ferrules & Manitoba Walnut

When commercial lock picks lack acoustic feedback and ergonomic grip during marathon bench decoding sessions, you make your own. Here is the machining and finishing process for our bespoke Westman pick set.

DISPATCHED: Monday, Sep 14, 2026
•
READ TIME: 3 min (627 words)
•
LOCATION: 📍 Westman Bench Workshop
•
TEMP: +21°C (Late Summer Sun)

In the trade, tools are an extension of your nervous system. When you are picking a serrated spool pin in a tight European keyway, your eyes can’t see the shear line; you are relying 100% on tactile friction and microscopic acoustic resonance transmitted up through the pick shank into your fingertips.

Most commercial mass-manufactured picks use cheap stamped steel with dipped rubber or plastic handles that dampen vibration. Over the past month in the wood and metal shop, I set out to produce a set of heirloom-grade tension tools and picks combining 301 High-Yield full-hard stainless steel, solid C360 free-machining brass bolsters, and salvaged Manitoba black walnut scales.

Material Selection & Metallurgy

⚡

MATERIALS BILL // ARTISAN PICK BUILD

BENCH LOG
  • Blade Stock: 301 High-Yield Stainless Steel Strip (0.019" / 0.48mm and 0.015" / 0.38mm)
  • Yield Strength: 270,000+ PSI (Extreme flexural memory without taking a set)
  • Handle Bolsters: C360 Brass round bar stock, lathe-turned and contoured
  • Scales: Manitoba Black Walnut, seasoned 4 years, stabilized in cactus juice resin
  • Fasteners: Solid copper rod rivets (peened by hand over countersunk brass washers)
  • Tool Roll: 5 oz Wickett & Craig harness saddle leather, hand saddle-stitched with 0.8mm waxed poly cord

Why 301 High-Yield? Unlike standard 304 or 400-series stainless, 301 HY undergoes extreme cold rolling that induces high dislocation density in the crystal lattice. When a pick blade enters a paracentric keyway and bends around warding, 301 HY snaps back dead true without ductile deformation.

The Machining Process

1. Profiling the Blades

The blanks were shaped using a low-speed water-cooled diamond wheel grinder to prevent heat buildup. Annealing the steel by overheating would destroy the spring temper.

The three core profiles shaped for this run:

  • The Short Flat Hook: 0.019" thickness for standard American pin tumblers.
  • The Deep Reach Gem: 0.015" thickness with a subtle 15-degree teardrop tip for navigating high-low bittings behind deep warding.
  • The Micro Quint Rake: Gently radiused cycloidal waves for rapid raking of legacy wafer cylinders and padlock cores.

2. Laser Graduation Marks

Using our bench fiber laser, I etched microscopic millimeter depth index marks (1mm to 6mm) directly along the pick shank. When working inside a dark keyway with zero external visibility, these index lines allow you to instantly determine whether your pick tip is resting on pin 3 or pin 4 simply by looking at the cylinder plug face.

3. Turning the Brass Ferrules & Fitting Walnut Scales

The brass ferrules were turned on the shop mini-lathe to a seamless rounded shoulder that transitions smoothly from the metal pick shank into the walnut handle.

The walnut wood scales were rough-cut, drilled with a carbide drill press, and pinned with solid copper rivets. After peening the rivet heads flush, each handle was hand-sanded from 220 grit through 2000 grit, followed by three coats of boiled linseed oil and buffed with carnauba wax.

💡 THE ACOUSTIC ADVANTAGE OF BRASS & SOLID WOOD
Unlike hollow plastic or silicone handles, dense stabilized walnut clamped between brass bolsters creates a rigid acoustic bridge. When a driver pin clicks into a false set or snaps past a serration, the micro-vibration resonates sharply against your palm and fingertips, making lock decoding almost intuitive.

Crafting the Leather Roll

A good set of precision tools deserves proper armor against the prairie elements.

I cut a single piece of 5 oz English bridle leather, grooved stitch lines, and punched chisel holes at 4mm spacing. Using two harness needles and traditional two-handed saddle stitching, the roll features 8 custom-sized pockets for the pick shanks, plus two dedicated sleeves for knurled top-of-keyway (TOK) prybars.

This tool roll now lives in my field pouch, ready for both delicate laboratory safe-lock teardowns and urgent rural service calls.
