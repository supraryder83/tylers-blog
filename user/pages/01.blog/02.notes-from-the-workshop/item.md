There is a distinct difference between writing code and cutting a piece of metal or wood. In software, when an error occurs, you hit undo or recompile. The feedback loop is immediate, forgiving, and virtually costless.

When you are cutting material in the shop, every cut is permanent. If you take off too much stock, you cannot put it back. The material demands your undivided presence.

### The Value of Friction

We often spend so much time optimizing for convenience that we forget how much satisfaction comes from friction. Measuring twice with a pair of calipers, setting up a dial indicator, or carefully tuning a toolpath forces you to slow down. 

That deliberate pace changes how you think. You start to anticipate failure modes before they happen. You respect tolerances. You begin to appreciate the invisible craftsmanship in everyday manufactured objects that most people walk past without a second glance.

### Starting Small

Whenever I start a new project, my instinct used to be to plan out a grand, complicated system. Over the years, I have learned that the best projects almost always start as simple prototypes:

1. Prove the core concept with whatever materials are currently on the bench.
2. Measure the actual outcome against what you expected.
3. Iterate only where the prototype fell short.

Keep the momentum moving, but keep the standard high.
